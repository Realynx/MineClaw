# Modular mine layouts

The Mine Designer offers six reusable excavation pieces: a narrow exploration tunnel, a branch network, a chamber, a straight stair descent, a spiral shaft and a four-way junction. Click a card and then the mine, or drag its card onto the plot. The pointer projects onto the selected **entrance feet Y**, independently of the display cutaway. Pieces are centred around the pointer rather than growing entirely toward one edge.

Select a piece to rotate, resize, move or remove it. Dimensions commit on blur or Enter so typing a new number does not repeatedly rebuild the mine. Nearby opposing entrances on the same feet level snap to adjacent cells; the snapping does not silently change the chosen height. A piece must fit completely inside the 64 × 64 plot and Y1–127 and may not excavate bedrock. Placement refuses invalid geometry instead of clipping it.

## Selecting and navigating

The **Components** outliner stays at the top right, above the scrolling properties and layout palette. It lists existing layout pieces with their coordinates. Click a row to select it through terrain, then use its axis handles, arrow keys / PgUp / PgDn, or the inspector's X/Y/Z fields to move it. Selecting another component reveals its inspector without scrolling the page. Coordinate edits commit on blur or Enter; Escape cancels the draft. Double-click a row or use the frame button to centre the camera on the whole component. With the canvas focused, **F** or **Numpad .** frames the selection.

Mine Designer keeps the cursor visible. **Middle-drag orbits**, **Shift + middle-drag pans**, and the **wheel zooms** around the focus point. **Alt + left-drag** and **Shift + Alt + left-drag** offer the same orbit and pan gestures without a middle button. Plain left clicks select components or use the selected excavation tool; missing a movement handle never enters immersive freecam. Handles keep a readable screen size while zooming. Builder retains its click-to-lock, WASD freecam controls.

The list represents layout components created with the palette, including locally recovered saved components. Manual excavation and older cell-only presets remain editable cells rather than guessed component groups.

## Layout choices and evidence

The palette separates extraction from access. Narrow tunnels expose fresh rock at low excavation cost; branch networks organize that exploration around a shared corridor. Chambers and junctions are connection and working space, not yield optimizations. Straight stairs and compact spirals provide stepped access with three blocks of clearance. The defaults leave a solid supporting block under each descent path cell.

The project's [mine layout study](mine-blueprints.md) remains the source for the default four-solid-block branch gap and its beta/1.12 ore targets. Its measured results apply to MineClaw's own terrain simulator, not an exact reproduction of a vanilla seed. A gap counts the solid blocks between branches: four means a branch every fifth block. Small gaps trade more excavation for more coverage. The palette exposes that setting instead of implying one universally optimal pattern.

Research checked on 2026-11-08:

- Philip Golden's original [Mining with the Agent: Part II](https://education.minecraft.net/en-us/lessons/mining-with-the-agent-part-ii), hosted by Minecraft Education and updated June 2018, explicitly compares straight and spiral staircase shafts, proposes branch patterns for finding grouped ores and suggests an underground crafting base to reduce repeated surface trips. This supports offering distinct access, extraction and hub pieces rather than one oversized mining form. The associated ore chart is used as a planning aid, not a guarantee of yield.
- Minecraft Education's [Coding with Minecraft 7: Iteration student workbook](https://education.minecraft.net/content/dam/education-edition/learning-experiences/coding_with_minecraft/Coding%20with%20Minecraft%207-Iteration-Student%20workbook.pdf), page 23, illustrates a spiral descent and three-block column removal at each turn. Its preceding straight-stair exercise discusses widening the passage and retaining headroom. The palette adopts those geometry families, while its exact dimensions and supporting-floor checks come from the local generators and regression tests.
- Mojang's [Java Edition 1.18 release notes](https://feedback.minecraft.net/hc/en-us/articles/4415128577293-Minecraft-Java-Edition-1-18) explicitly describe changed world height and ore distribution. This is why modern negative-Y advice must not replace the existing beta 1.8.1 and 1.12.2 tables. The designer keeps those versions' practical feet targets: Y11 for diamond/redstone/gold, Y16 for lapis/iron and Y48 for coal. These practical targets are not all density peaks.
- Samomezz's [original mining simulation repository](https://github.com/samomezz/minecraftStripMiningSimulation) publishes code, data and limitations for a spacing study across 14 worlds. It samples only four vertical blocks around modern Y−59/−58 and reports a plateau around five to six solid blocks. Its version, height and limited vein sampling differ from this project's study, so it supports adjustable gaps and a coverage/effort tradeoff, not replacing the local four-block default or old-version heights.

The coloured ore-height chart remains visible and can collapse. Ore chips set entrance height; the chart and cutaway slider change the view only. Planned excavation appears in cyan. Amber outlines show each entire face-connected ore vein discovered in or directly beside that excavation, including visible floor ore and segments near hazards. Unrelated seed caves and ore remain hidden, and authored blocks replace the ore underneath them. This is a discovery preview, not permission or a guarantee to excavate every outlined cell: miners still protect access floors and supports and apply their normal ownership and hazard checks. Yield estimates are illustrative; miners may follow eligible connected ore beyond the authored excavation.

## State, overlap and undo

`mine/modules.ts` produces ordinary authored excavation cells, entrance ports and snapping. `MineModuleController` keeps the instances and each covered cell's previous authored state. Moving or removing a piece recomputes the union of the remaining pieces. Shared tunnels remain, older manual excavation is restored and subsequent manual exceptions such as a torch or a filled block are retained. The mine canvas distinguishes untouched natural air from explicitly authored excavation so tunnels crossing caves remain part of the plan.

Studio history attaches undo/redo metadata to the same operation as the cells, including a completely overlapping piece that changes no cells. Repeated moves with one gesture identifier coalesce into one undo step. A move combined with changed dimensions is committed atomically.

Instance metadata is saved locally per owner/session/version/seed. After a successful design save, it is also copied under that design revision, allowing a new designer session in the same browser to recover movable pieces. Recovery requires every owned cell to match the loaded canvas (including manual exceptions); stale metadata is discarded and never changes the canvas. The server stores the ordinary excavation and placed cells. Piece grouping is currently browser-local and does not travel to another device. Existing saved mines and version presets remain usable as ordinary canvas cells without inferred groups.

Regression coverage in `mineModules.test.ts` checks all six layouts and rotations, entrance clearance, descent floors, compatible snapping, bounds refusal, overlaps, manual edits, metadata-only undo, grouped drags and validated metadata recovery. `studioMine.test.tsx` covers the palette, drag payloads, separate entrance/view heights and the version-specific guide.
